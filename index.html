01
Where it started

Before the
code, there
was a broken
radio.

I was eight when I took apart a radio — not to fix it, but because I needed to know how sound could live inside wires. I never fixed it. But I remember staring at the components spread across the floor and feeling something click into place. Not in the radio. In me.

I grew up in a household where curiosity was encouraged even when it was inconvenient. By the time I reached high school, that curiosity had a direction: I wanted to build things that were useful to real people, not just impressive in a lab. I became school council member, captained the interhouse football team — and somewhere in the middle of learning to lead eleven people on a field, I understood something about systems: they're only as good as the communication inside them.

Engineering wasn't a career choice for me. It was the answer to a question I'd been carrying for years: what's the fastest path from a problem that matters to a solution that works?

02
Building real things

From
curiosity
to craft.

There's a gap between knowing how something works and building something that works for someone. Crossing that gap — repeatedly, imperfectly, sometimes publicly — is what the last few years have been about.

IoT · Smart Infrastructure

Road Condition Monitoring System

My first project that felt genuinely useful. Sensors on buses, real-time pothole detection, data pipelines feeding into infrastructure dashboards. I took it all the way — research paper, grant proposal, an interview with our Vice Chancellor. The first prototype had a sensor accuracy issue that took three weeks to debug. I learned more in those three weeks than in a semester of labs.

Drones · Aeromodelling Club

Building My First Drone from Scratch

I joined the UEM Aeromodelling Club and attended the UDAAN workshop by Dronesster. Building a drone from component parts — frame, flight controller, ESCs, PID tuning — taught me something that software alone never could: physics doesn't have exceptions. If the math is wrong, the drone falls. I've thought about that a lot when debugging ML models.

Competition · Technex IIT BHU 2026

Stellar Analytics — NASA Kepler Dataset

My team Brahmastra entered Stellar Analytics at IIT BHU's Technex with an ML pipeline for exoplanet transit classification — LightGBM, voting ensembles, a neural network regressor. We hit ROC-AUC 0.9504 and R² of 0.9635. We won first place. Winning felt less like a destination and more like a green light — to push it further, toward a paper, toward diffusion models for signal denoising.

Quant Finance · AlgoZen

Building a Trading System I'd Actually Trust

AlgoZen wasn't built in a weekend. It went through multiple full architectural rebuilds — regime detection, walk-forward optimization, genetic algorithm tuning on NIFTY 500. The targets were specific: Sharpe above 1.0, CAGR 14–18%, max drawdown under 15%. When it finally hit Sharpe 1.129, I understood why quantitative people are obsessive. The discipline the problem demands becomes the discipline you develop.

03
Machine learning & quantitative thinking

Patterns
in the
noise.

The transition from building physical systems to thinking about intelligent ones wasn't a decision — it was gravitational. Climate data, satellite photometry, financial time series: I kept running into the same observation. The most important problems in the world are already being measured. They're just not being understood well enough yet.

ML gave me a different kind of engineering. Not one where you define every rule, but one where you define the space for rules to emerge. There's something almost philosophical about that — and I'm not sure I've fully separated the philosophy from the practice, or that I want to.

Quantitative finance drew me in for the same reason. Making better decisions under uncertainty, with incomplete information, in real time — that's not just a finance problem. It's the same shape as almost every hard problem I find interesting. Right now I'm exploring diffusion models for exoplanet signal denoising, which is a very specific sentence that would have meant nothing to me two years ago and now feels like the most natural next step in the world.

I don't think I fell in love with technology.
I think I fell in love with the feeling of making
something useful exist where nothing existed before —
and the discipline it takes to get there honestly.
04
Community & leadership

The best
things I've
built were
with people.

I captained a football team before I ever led a technical project. That experience gave me something no CS curriculum covers: people don't follow plans, they follow conviction. On the field I learned to read a group's energy, to know when to push and when to step back, to understand that leadership isn't about being the most talented person in the room.

Co-organizing ACEHack 5.0 — 10,000+ registrations — was a different kind of test. The logistics were hard. But what I remember most are the moments: someone's code finally working at 3 AM, strangers becoming collaborators in a corridor, a first-time participant realizing they could actually compete. I did it partly because I remembered what it felt like to be the underdog in a room full of people more experienced than me — and how much it mattered that the room existed at all.

Volunteering at the International Conference on Advances in English Language Studies grounded me in a different way. It reminded me that the most interesting conversations happen at the edges of disciplines — and that the people doing the most thoughtful work are rarely the loudest ones in the room.
