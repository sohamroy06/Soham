01
Where it started

Before the
code, there
was a broken
radio.

I was eight when I took apart a radio — not to fix it, but to see how sound could live inside wires. I never fixed it. I still remember the components spread across the floor and the quiet click it made in me.

Curiosity shaped most of my early years. By high school, it had become something more focused: a desire to build technology that felt useful, grounded, and human. I became a school council member, captained the interhouse football team, and learned that leadership and engineering are not all that different. Both depend on people understanding one another clearly.

The quality of a system is often defined by the quality of communication within it.

Engineering never felt like just a career path to me.
It felt like the cleanest way to turn curiosity into something useful.

Building real things

From
curiosity

There's a gap between knowing how something works and building something that works for someone. Crossing that gap — repeatedly, imperfectly — has been the work.

IoT · Smart Infrastructure

Road Condition Monitoring System

My first project that felt genuinely useful. Sensors on buses, real-time pothole detection, and infrastructure dashboards. I took it all the way — paper, grant proposal, and an interview with our Vice Chancellor. The first prototype had a sensor accuracy issue that took three weeks to debug.

Drones · Aeromodelling Club

Building My First Drone from Scratch

I joined the UEM Aeromodelling Club and attended the UDAAN workshop by Dronesster. Building a drone from component parts — frame, flight controller, ESCs, PID tuning — taught me something software alone never could: physics doesn't have exceptions. If the math is wrong, the drone falls.

LinkedIn post

Competition · Technex IIT BHU 2025

Case Study Challenge — Top 3

At Technex IIT BHU, my team entered a national-level case study competition that demanded more than technical thinking. We had to break down ambiguity, build a structured solution, and defend it in front of a panel — all within limited time. We finished in the top 3.

Competition · Technex IIT BHU 2026

Stellar Analytics — NASA Kepler Dataset

My team Brahmastra entered Stellar Analytics at IIT BHU's Technex with an ML pipeline for exoplanet transit classification — LightGBM, voting ensembles, and a neural network regressor. We hit ROC-AUC 0.9504 and R² of 0.9635, then took first place.

Live GitHub

Quant Finance · AlgoZen

Building a Trading System I'd Actually Trust

AlgoZen wasn't built in a weekend. It went through multiple architectural rebuilds — regime detection, walk-forward optimization, genetic algorithm tuning on NIFTY 500. The targets were specific: Sharpe above 1.0, CAGR 14–18%, max drawdown under 15%. When it finally hit Sharpe 1.129, I understood why quantitative people are obsessive.

03
Machine learning & quantitative thinking

Patterns
in the
noise.

Moving from physical systems to intelligent ones felt less like a decision than gravity. Climate data, satellite photometry, financial time series: the most important problems are already being measured. They're just not being understood well enough yet.

ML gave me a different kind of engineering. Not one where you define every rule, but one where you define the space for rules to emerge. Quant finance pulled on the same thread — making better decisions under uncertainty, in real time.

Right now I'm exploring diffusion models for exoplanet signal denoising, which is a very specific sentence that would have meant nothing to me two years ago and now feels like the obvious next step.

04
Community & leadership

The best
things I've
built were
with people.

Long before I led technical projects, I was learning how people work under pressure. That still shapes how I think about leadership: less about being the smartest person in the room, more about creating clarity, trust, and momentum.

Co-organizing ACEHack 5.0 — 10,000+ registrations — was the real test. The logistics were hard, but what stayed with me were the moments: someone's code finally working at 3 AM, strangers becoming collaborators, a first-time participant realizing they could actually compete.

Volunteering at the International Conference on Advances in English Language Studies reminded me that the best conversations happen at the edges of disciplines — and that thoughtful work is rarely the loudest work in the room.
