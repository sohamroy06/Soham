01
The challenge

NASA's Kepler mission produced years of stellar brightness data, hunting for the tiny, periodic dips in light that hint at a planet passing in front of its star. Most of those dips aren't planets — they're noise, eclipsing binaries, or instrumental artifacts. Telling the difference at scale, and doing it inside a competition's time limit, was the problem Technex IIT BHU 2026 handed our team, Brahmastra, in the Stellar Analytics challenge.

02
Approach

We split the problem in two: a classification model to separate genuine transit candidates from false positives, and a regression model to estimate the parameters of the ones that looked real. For classification, we used LightGBM wrapped in a voting ensemble — a single gradient-boosted tree was fast but brittle on the harder edge cases, and averaging across several reduced that variance without a heavy cost in inference time.

For the regression side, we trained a neural network, since the relationships between the raw signal features and the physical parameters we were estimating weren't clean enough for a simpler model to capture reliably.

03
Challenges

The dataset was heavily imbalanced — confirmed exoplanets are a small fraction of all candidate signals — so accuracy alone would have been a meaningless metric. We optimized for ROC-AUC instead, which forced us to actually get the ranking of uncertain cases right rather than just calling everything "not a planet." The other constraint was time: this was a live competition, not a semester project, so every architecture decision had to be one we could implement, validate, and defend to a panel within the window we had.

04
Where it's going

Stellar Analytics is also the project I'm still living in. I'm extending it into a proper paper — using diffusion models to denoise exoplanet transit signals before classification, rather than fighting the noise inside the classifier itself. I'm targeting NeurIPS's ML4PS workshop and the Astronomical Journal. It's the first time a competition project has turned into something I'm trying to publish, which changes how I think about the six-week version of a project versus the six-month one.
